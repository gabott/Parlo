## 1. Unit promise

### Learner-facing introduction

> Welcome to your first French unit. You will learn how to greet someone, spell a name, recognize several important French sounds, be polite, and ask for help when you do not understand. You do not need to sound native. Your goal is to communicate clearly and recognize the expressions when another person uses them.

### Capability

**I can recognize key French sounds, greet someone, and use basic classroom survival expressions.**

### Unit completion requirements

- Complete Lessons 1–5.
- Pass each lesson exit check at 70% or higher with its required recall evidence.
- Complete the integrated review.
- Pass the Unit 1 capability assessment at 70% or higher.
- Complete the speaking tasks at rubric level 2 or higher through structured self-review for the initial deterministic release.

### Estimated time

- Five lessons: 15–20 minutes each.
- Integrated review: 15–20 minutes.
- Capability assessment: 15–20 minutes.
- Spaced review outside the unit: approximately 20–30 minutes distributed over later days.

## 2. Scope and content limits

Unit 1 introduces communication chunks rather than teaching their complete grammar. Learners may encounter `vous`, negation, an imperative, and a question form as useful expressions. Their grammatical systems are taught later.

The unit does not require:

- Memorizing the International Phonetic Alphabet.
- Producing a native-like French `r`.
- Mastering every French sound-spelling rule.
- Conjugating verbs.
- Explaining formal grammar terminology.
- Completing open-ended AI conversation.

## 6. Unit feedback style

Use these rules throughout Unit 1:

- Lead with meaning and the corrective action.
- Explain one relevant point at a time.
- Replay the correct audio after an error when sound is relevant.
- Offer one immediate retry, then schedule delayed review.
- Accept straight and curly apostrophes.
- Ignore final punctuation unless punctuation is being taught.
- Treat missing accent marks as `correct_with_note` in communication activities and as incorrect in spelling-focused items.
- Never penalize a learner for a non-native accent if the response is intelligible.

Standard feedback:

| Situation | Learner-facing feedback |
|---|---|
| Correct | Correct. |
| Correct with accent note | Your message is correct. In careful writing, keep the accent: **{target}**. |
| Day/evening confusion | Use **bonjour** during the day and **bonsoir** in the evening. |
| Greeting/farewell confusion | **{answer}** is used when {context}. |
| Formality mismatch | **Salut** is informal. Use **bonjour** in this polite or unfamiliar situation. |
| Spelling mismatch | Listen once more and compare the letters in order. |
| Sound discrimination error | Listen to the contrast again. Focus on **{articulation cue}**. |
| Communication repair error | Use **{answer}** to ask the other person to {purpose}. |

## 7. Lesson 1 — Greetings and farewells

**ID:** `lesson.fr-general.a1.first-contact.greetings`  
**Matrix ID:** `A1-U01-L01`  
**Estimated time:** 15 minutes  
**Objective:** I can choose and use an appropriate greeting or farewell.

### 7.1 Opening context

**Scene:** Two adult learners arrive for a morning class. Later, they leave.

**Dialogue A — arriving**

| Speaker | French | English support |
|---|---|---|
| Sofia | Bonjour ! | Hello! |
| Amir | Bonjour ! | Hello! |
| Sofia | Ça va ? | How are you? |
| Amir | Ça va bien, merci. | I'm well, thank you. |

**Dialogue B — leaving**

| Speaker | French | English support |
|---|---|---|
| Sofia | Au revoir ! | Goodbye! |
| Amir | À demain ! Bonne journée ! | See you tomorrow! Have a good day! |

Presentation behavior:

1. Play full audio without transcript.
2. Ask: “Are they arriving or leaving?”
3. Replay with synchronized transcript.
4. Allow line-by-line audio.

### 7.2 Teaching copy

> Use **bonjour** to greet someone during the day. Use **bonsoir** to greet someone in the evening. **Salut** means “hi” and can also mean “bye,” but it is informal. When leaving, **au revoir** is a safe general choice.

> **Bonne journée** and **bonne soirée** are wishes usually said when leaving: “Have a good day/evening.” They are not the same as **bonjour** and **bonsoir**, which are greetings.

### 7.3 Notice activity

Prompt: “Which expressions did you hear when the learners arrived?”

Options:

- `Bonjour !` — correct.
- `Au revoir !`
- `À demain !`

Feedback: “They say **bonjour** when they meet during the day.”

### 7.4 Meaning-selection items

| Item ID | Situation | Options | Answer | Error-specific feedback |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `u01l01-ms-001` | It is 9:00 a.m. You greet your teacher. | Bonjour / Bonsoir / Au revoir | Bonjour | `Bonsoir`: evening; `Au revoir`: leaving. |
| `u01l01-ms-002` | You leave a store. | Au revoir / Bonjour / Salut | Au revoir | `Bonjour`: arrival/daytime greeting; `Salut`: informal. |
| `u01l01-ms-003` | You greet a close friend informally. | Salut / Bonne journée / Au revoir | Salut | `Bonne journée`: leaving wish; `Au revoir`: goodbye. |
| `u01l01-ms-004` | It is evening and you enter a restaurant. | Bonsoir / Bonne soirée / À demain | Bonsoir | `Bonne soirée`: normally a leaving wish. |
| `u01l01-ms-005` | You will see your classmate tomorrow. | À demain / À bientôt / Bonjour | À demain | `À bientôt`: soon, but not specifically tomorrow. |
| `u01l01-ms-006` | You leave during the day and wish someone well. | Bonne journée / Bonjour / Bonsoir | Bonne journée | Use it as a daytime leaving wish. |

Select four per lesson attempt, balanced across greeting, farewell, time, and register.

### 7.5 Listening selection

Four items play one expression and offer three written choices. Required contrasts:

1. `Bonjour` / `Bonne journée` / `Bonsoir`
2. `Au revoir` / `À demain` / `À bientôt`
3. `Bonsoir` / `Bonne soirée` / `Bonjour`
4. `Salut` / `S'il vous plaît` / `Merci`

Correct answer requires text selection only; spelling is not tested here.

### 7.6 Sentence builder

| Prompt | Tokens | Accepted result |
|---|---|---|
| Build “Have a good day!” | `Bonne` `journée` `!` | `Bonne journée !` |
| Build “See you tomorrow!” | `À` `demain` `!` | `À demain !` |
| Build a daytime greeting. | `Bonjour` `!` | `Bonjour !` |

Punctuation is an explicit token for learning display but does not affect scoring.

### 7.7 Guided speaking

Prompt A: “It is morning. Record a greeting for your teacher.”  
Expected function: daytime polite greeting.  
Model after recording: `Bonjour !`

Prompt B: “You are leaving and will return tomorrow. Record your farewell.”  
Expected function: farewell + tomorrow.  
Model: `Au revoir ! À demain !`

Self-review checklist:

- Did I use the expression appropriate to the situation?
- Could I understand my words when I replayed them?
- Did I speak the phrase as one short group?

### 7.8 Exit check

Four unseen/alternate items:

1. Listening recognition: evening greeting.
2. Context choice: polite daytime arrival.
3. Typed recall: “See you soon!” → `À bientôt !`
4. Guided speaking: daytime farewell wish → `Bonne journée !`

Pass: three of four, with at least one recall/production item successful. Speaking uses structured self-review in the initial release and must be submitted.

### 7.9 Review enrollment

Enter the eight core greeting/farewell phrases into review. Initial productive priority: `bonjour`, `bonsoir`, `au revoir`, `salut`; remaining phrases begin with recognition then recall.

## 8. Lesson 2 — Names and alphabet

**ID:** `lesson.fr-general.a1.first-contact.alphabet-spelling`  
**Matrix ID:** `A1-U01-L02`  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Objective:** I can recognize French letter names and spell a first name.

### 8.1 Opening context

**Scene:** A learner gives their name at a class desk.

| Speaker | French | English support |
|---|---|---|
| Receptionist | Bonjour. Votre prénom ? | Hello. Your first name? |
| Learner | Chloé. | Chloé. |
| Receptionist | Comment ça s'écrit ? | How is that spelled? |
| Learner | C–H–L–O–É. | C–H–L–O–É. |
| Receptionist | Merci. | Thank you. |

`Votre prénom ?` is recognition-only here. Names and introductions are taught fully in Unit 2.

### 8.2 Alphabet reference

| Letter | Learner-friendly cue | Letter | Learner-friendly cue |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | ah | N | enn |
| B | bay | O | oh |
| C | say | P | pay |
| D | day | Q | kü |
| E | uh | R | French air |
| F | eff | S | ess |
| G | zhay | T | tay |
| H | ahsh | U | French u |
| I | ee | V | vay |
| J | zhee | W | doo-bluh-vay |
| K | kah | X | eeks |
| L | ell | Y | ee-grek |
| M | emm | Z | zed |

The cues are temporary listening aids, not exact English pronunciations. Each row must include reviewed French audio. Do not display cues in spelling assessments.

### 8.3 Teaching copy

> French uses the same 26 basic letters as English, but many letter names sound different. Listen especially for **G/J**, **E/I**, and **Q/U**.

> French also uses marks such as **é, è, ê, ë, à, ç, î, ô**. These are not extra alphabet letters, but the marks matter in careful writing. In a name, keep the person's spelling exactly.

### 8.4 Alphabet learning rounds

Divide the alphabet into four rounds to control load:

1. A–F
2. G–M
3. N–T
4. U–Z

Each round:

1. Listen and repeat the sequence.
2. Select the written letter after audio.
3. Select the audio after seeing the letter.
4. Complete two-letter and three-letter dictations.

### 8.5 Contrast items

Required minimal alphabet-listening contrasts:

- G / J
- E / I
- B / P
- D / T
- Q / U
- M / N
- C / Z

Each item plays one letter twice at learner speed and once at natural spelling speed. Options remain visible.

### 8.6 Name dictation bank

| Item ID | Audio spelling | Accepted written name | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| `u01l02-di-001` | L–É–A | Léa | Accent preservation |
| `u01l02-di-002` | A–M–I–R | Amir | Four-letter sequence |
| `u01l02-di-003` | N–O–A–H | Noah | Vowel sequence |
| `u01l02-di-004` | S–O–F–I–A | Sofia | Five-letter sequence |
| `u01l02-di-005` | H–U–G–O | Hugo | H/U/G contrast |
| `u01l02-di-006` | I–N–È–S | Inès | Grave accent |
| `u01l02-di-007` | C–H–L–O–É | Chloé | Longer sequence/accent |
| `u01l02-di-008` | S–A–M–I–R | Samir | M/N discrimination context |

Spelling-focused evaluation requires accent marks where present. After one accent-only error, show the correction and allow a focused retry.

### 8.7 Phrase practice

1. Audio → select `Comment ça s'écrit ?`
2. Build `Ça s'écrit…`
3. Audio → select `Pouvez-vous épeler, s'il vous plaît ?`
4. Record `Ça s'écrit…` followed by the learner's chosen name.

For personal-name recording, retain audio according to private learner settings and never add the name to public content.

### 8.8 Exit check

1. Three audio-letter selections using high-confusion pairs.
2. One four- or five-letter name dictation.
3. Typed recall: “How is that spelled?” → `Comment ça s'écrit ?`
4. Spoken spelling task using a fictional displayed name.

Pass: 70% overall, at least 70% on letter recognition, and successful ordered spelling of one name. Accent-only error may be corrected once but is recorded for review.

### 8.9 Review enrollment

- Alphabet pairs with observed confusion enter short-interval review.
- Spelling phrases enter phrase review.
- The entire alphabet does not become 26 identical daily flashcards; review targets confused letters and spelling sequences.

## 9. Lesson 3 — Core sound map

**ID:** `lesson.fr-general.a1.first-contact.sound-map`  
**Matrix ID:** `A1-U01-L03`  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Objective:** I can hear differences among several important French sounds and make a clear first attempt.

### 9.1 Learner-facing introduction

> French spelling does not map to English sounds. In this lesson, train your ears first. You are not expected to perfect these sounds today. Listen, compare, and make a comfortable first attempt.

### 9.2 Sound target A — `u` and `ou`

Teaching copy:

> For French **ou**, round your lips and say a sound close to the vowel in English “food.” For French **u**, keep your tongue in a position close to “ee” while rounding your lips. Start slowly; the goal is to hear that they are different.

Reviewed example set:

| `u` target | Meaning | `ou` target | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| `tu` | you, informal | `tout` | all/everything |
| `rue` | street | `roue` | wheel |
| `dessus` | on top | `dessous` | underneath |

Only `tu` has near-term curricular importance; other examples demonstrate sound and do not enter core vocabulary review.

Activities:

- Six audio discrimination items: choose `u` or `ou` column.
- Three text-to-audio selections.
- Record and compare `tu — tout`, then `rue — roue`.

### 9.3 Sound target B — `é` and `è`

Teaching copy:

> **é** is a more closed vowel, heard in words such as **café**. **è** is more open, heard in words such as **père**. Several spellings can represent these sounds; today, focus on listening.

Example set:

| `é` | Meaning | `è` | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| `café` | coffee/café | `père` | father |
| `été` | summer | `mère` | mother |
| `répétez` | repeat | `très` | very |

Activities: six discrimination items, three audio-to-text items, and two record-and-compare prompts.

### 9.4 Sound target C — nasal-vowel awareness

Teaching copy:

> In many French words, a vowel followed by **n** or **m** creates a nasal vowel. Air passes partly through the nose, and the written **n/m** is often not pronounced as a separate final consonant. Listen to the three groups; exact spelling rules come later.

Example groups:

| `an/en` | `on` | `in/ain` |
|---|---|---|
| `sans`, `enfant` | `nom`, `bonjour` | `cinq`, `matin` |

Activities:

- Nine three-way sorting items.
- Three “same or different?” audio pairs.
- Record `sans — son — cinq` slowly, then listen to models.

Do not assign automated production correctness in the deterministic release. Completion requires recording/playback and self-review.

### 9.5 Sound target D — French `r`

Teaching copy:

> The common French **r** is made toward the back of the mouth. It does not need to be rolled. A light or imperfect sound is acceptable if the word remains clear. Avoid forcing or hurting your throat.

Examples: `rue`, `merci`, `répétez`, `au revoir`.

Activity: listen, then record `merci` and `au revoir`. Self-review asks only whether the word is recognizable and comfortable.

### 9.6 Rhythm

Teaching copy:

> French phrases tend to flow in groups. Avoid giving every written word a heavy English-style stress. Listen and echo the whole group:

- `Bonjour !`
- `Merci beaucoup.`
- `À demain !`
- `Au revoir !`

Shadow each phrase twice: once at learner speed and once at natural speed.

### 9.7 Exit check

Ten recognition items:

- Three `u/ou`.
- Two `é/è`.
- Three nasal-vowel group selections.
- Two phrase/rhythm audio matches.

Plus one required record-and-playback reflection using `Bonjour. Merci beaucoup. Au revoir.`

Pass: at least 7/10 recognition items and completion of the production reflection. No native-likeness score.

### 9.8 Review enrollment

Only missed or low-confidence contrasts enter targeted review. Sound items rotate example words to reduce memorization of recordings.

## 10. Lesson 4 — Courtesy

**ID:** `lesson.fr-general.a1.first-contact.courtesy`  
**Matrix ID:** `A1-U01-L04`  
**Estimated time:** 15 minutes  
**Objective:** I can use basic polite expressions appropriately.

### 10.1 Opening context

**Scene:** A learner enters class late and asks for a handout.

| Speaker | French | English support |
|---|---|---|
| Learner | Bonjour. Excusez-moi. | Hello. Excuse me. |
| Teacher | Bonjour. | Hello. |
| Learner | Le document, s'il vous plaît. | The handout, please. |
| Teacher | Voilà. | Here you are. |
| Learner | Merci beaucoup. | Thank you very much. |
| Teacher | De rien. | You're welcome. |

`Le document` and `Voilà` are contextual recognition only.

### 10.2 Teaching copy

> **Merci** is the everyday way to say “thank you.” Add **beaucoup** for “thank you very much.” A common response is **de rien**.

> Use **s'il vous plaît** for “please” when speaking politely to one person or to more than one person. **Excusez-moi** gets someone's attention or introduces a polite interruption. **Pardon** is useful for a brief apology, such as bumping into someone.

> You will learn the complete difference between **tu** and **vous** later. For now, the `vous` phrases in this unit are safe polite choices with a teacher or unfamiliar adult.

### 10.3 Context selection

| Item ID | Situation | Answer | Main contrast |
|---|---|---|---|
| `u01l04-ms-001` | Someone gives you a document. | Merci. | Thanks vs please |
| `u01l04-ms-002` | You politely ask for a coffee. | S'il vous plaît. | Request marker |
| `u01l04-ms-003` | You need to interrupt a teacher. | Excusez-moi. | Attention/polite interruption |
| `u01l04-ms-004` | You accidentally step on someone's foot. | Pardon. | Brief apology |
| `u01l04-ms-005` | Someone says `Merci`. | De rien. | Response pair |
| `u01l04-ms-006` | You are especially grateful. | Merci beaucoup. | Degree of thanks |

### 10.4 Listening and dictation

1. Audio selection: `merci` / `de rien` / `pardon`.
2. Audio selection: `merci beaucoup` / `bonjour` / `bonne journée`.
3. Dictation: `Merci.`
4. Dictation: `S'il vous plaît.`
5. Dictation: `Excusez-moi.`

Normalization accepts either apostrophe form. Hyphen and accent errors receive targeted orthography feedback.

### 10.5 Sentence builder

- `Excusez-moi` `.` → `Excusez-moi.`
- `Merci` `beaucoup` `.` → `Merci beaucoup.`
- `S'il` `vous` `plaît` `.` → `S'il vous plaît.`

The token `S'il` remains contracted and cannot be separated into `S'` + `il`.

### 10.6 Structured role-play

**Role-play A: classroom request**

1. Partner greets learner.
2. Learner selects or says a greeting.
3. Partner offers a choice of two classroom objects visually.
4. Learner requests one with `s'il vous plaît`.
5. Partner gives it.
6. Learner thanks partner.

**Role-play B: polite interruption**

1. Learner gets attention with `Excusez-moi`.
2. Partner turns/responds.
3. Learner uses a familiar request chunk.

Deterministic branches accept the intended communicative function, not only one full exact sentence.

### 10.7 Exit check

1. Context selection: brief accidental apology.
2. Listening response: hear `Merci` and select/say `De rien`.
3. Typed recall: “Excuse me” in a polite interruption.
4. Role-play: greeting + request marker + thanks.

Pass: three of four and successful completion of the role-play functions.

### 10.8 Review enrollment

All six courtesy phrases enter review, with paired/contextual prompts preferred over isolated translation.

| Learner | Pouvez-vous répéter, s'il vous plaît ? | Can you repeat, please? |
| Teacher | Oui. Écoutez… et répétez. | Yes. Listen… and repeat. |
| Learner | Plus lentement, s'il vous plaît. | More slowly, please. |
| Teacher | Bien sûr. | Of course. |
| Learner | Merci. Je comprends. | Thank you. I understand. |

`Bien sûr` is contextual recognition only.

### 11.2 Teaching copy

> Not understanding is normal. Use a repair phrase to keep the conversation going.

- **Je ne comprends pas.** — Say that you do not understand.
- **Pouvez-vous répéter, s'il vous plaît ?** — Ask someone politely to repeat.
- **Plus lentement, s'il vous plaît.** — Ask for slower speech.
- **Qu'est-ce que ça veut dire ?** — Ask what something means.
- **Comment dit-on ___ en français ?** — Ask how to say something in French.
- **Je comprends.** — Confirm that you understand.

> The words inside these expressions will be explained in later lessons. Learn each expression as one useful tool today.

### 11.3 Meaning/function matching

Match each problem to a strategy:

| Problem | Target response |
|---|---|
| You did not understand the message. | `Je ne comprends pas.` |
| You want to hear it again. | `Pouvez-vous répéter, s'il vous plaît ?` |
| The person is speaking too quickly. | `Plus lentement, s'il vous plaît.` |
| You want the meaning of a word. | `Qu'est-ce que ça veut dire ?` |
| You want the French word for “book.” | `Comment dit-on “book” en français ?` |
| You understand now. | `Je comprends.` |

### 11.4 Listening comprehension

**Micro-dialogue 1**

> A: Le cours commence à neuf heures.  
> B: Pardon. Plus lentement, s'il vous plaît.

Question: What does B need?  
Answer: Slower speech.

**Micro-dialogue 2**

> A: Ouvrez le document.  
> B: Je ne comprends pas. Qu'est-ce que “ouvrez” veut dire ?

Question: What is B asking for?  
Answer: The meaning of a word/instruction.

**Micro-dialogue 3**

> A: Votre cours est dans la salle douze.  
> B: Pouvez-vous répéter, s'il vous plaît ?

Question: What will A probably do next?  
Answer: Repeat the information.

Unknown contextual words do not need to be decoded; the task measures repair strategy recognition.

### 11.5 Sentence builders

1. `Je` `ne comprends pas` `.` → `Je ne comprends pas.`
2. `Pouvez-vous` `répéter` `,` `s'il vous plaît` `?`
3. `Plus lentement` `,` `s'il vous plaît` `.`
4. `Qu'est-ce que` `ça veut dire` `?`
5. `Comment dit-on` `{English word}` `en français` `?`

### 11.6 Dictation

- `Je comprends.`
- `Je ne comprends pas.`
- `Plus lentement, s'il vous plaît.`
- One of the two longer question phrases, with one replay at natural speed and unlimited learner-speed replay during instruction.

Spelling feedback prioritizes meaning-bearing chunks. Punctuation is advisory; apostrophes and accents are handled according to Unit 1 normalization rules.

### 11.7 Structured repair role-play

Round 1:

1. Partner says a familiar instruction quickly.
2. Learner asks for slower speech.
3. Partner repeats slowly.
4. Learner confirms understanding.

Round 2:

1. Partner uses one highlighted unknown word.
2. Learner asks what it means.
3. Partner gives English support or an image.
4. Learner thanks partner.

Round 3:

1. Partner says a familiar detail once.
2. Learner asks for repetition.
3. Partner repeats.

### 11.8 Exit check

1. Audio situation → select appropriate repair phrase.
2. Typed recall: “I don't understand.”
3. Sentence builder: polite repetition request.
4. Spoken role-play: request slower speech, then confirm understanding.

Pass: three of four, including the role-play functions. A learner who misses the long typed phrase may pass communicative evidence but receives orthography review.

### 11.9 Review enrollment

All six productive repair phrases enter spaced review. Longer phrases initially use supported recall, then sentence building, then typed/spoken recall.

## 12. Integrated review — First contact

**ID:** `lesson.fr-general.a1.first-contact.review`  
**Matrix ID:** `A1-U01-R01`  
**Estimated time:** 15–20 minutes  
**Objective:** I can retrieve Unit 1 language across listening, speaking, reading, and writing.

### 12.1 Dynamic composition

The review contains 14–18 items:

- 40% due or weak Unit 1 content.
- 20% greeting/courtesy context choices.
- 15% alphabet/spelling.
- 15% sound discrimination.
- 10% classroom-repair production.

Constraints:

- At least six recall/transfer items.
- At least four listening items.
- At least two speaking prompts.
- No identical prompt repeated from a lesson exit check.
- Answer exposure earlier in the same review reduces later recall evidence.

### 12.2 Fixed integrated scenario

**Scenario:** You arrive at an evening French class for the first time.

Sequence:

1. Select/say the evening greeting.
2. Hear a fictional name and type it.
3. Politely ask the speaker to spell a second name.
4. Respond after receiving a document.
5. Ask the teacher to repeat an instruction.
6. Ask for slower speech.
7. Confirm understanding.
8. Leave and use an evening farewell wish.

### 12.3 Review result

The learner sees:

- Expressions recalled independently.
- Sound/letter pairs needing more listening.
- Communication strategies ready for real use.
- Items entering later review.

There is no single “mastery” claim. The review reports evidence by concept/modality.

Completion requires submission of the fixed scenario and at least 70% on objective items. Failure produces a focused review set; it does not reset completed lessons.

## 13. Unit 1 capability assessment

**ID:** `assessment.fr-general.a1.first-contact`  
**Matrix ID:** `A1-U01-A01`  
**Estimated time:** 15–20 minutes  
**Capability:** I can recognize key French sounds, greet someone, and use basic classroom survival expressions.

### 13.1 Assessment conditions

- Uses unseen arrangements and recordings.
- No correctness feedback until submission.
- Instructions remain available.
- Learning accommodations remain active.
- Listening items allow two plays unless the item explicitly measures first-listen comprehension.
- Speaking uses recording and structured self-review in the deterministic foundation; it is not called an automated pronunciation score.
- Assessment may be retried after remediation with alternate Form B.

### 13.2 Form A blueprint

| Section | Items/tasks | Points | Required evidence |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Listening and sound recognition | 6 | 6 | Greeting/farewell, spelling, `u/ou`, `é/è`, nasal group |
| Reading/context | 4 | 4 | Choose appropriate polite or repair expression |
| Writing | 3 | 5 | Fictional name dictation; two short phrase recalls |
| Speaking/interaction | 1 scenario, 4 functions | 8 rubric/function points | Greet, request repeat/slow speech, thank, leave |
| **Total** | 14 items/tasks | **23** | At least one productive mode must pass |

Pass: 17/23 (74%, satisfying the ≥70% policy), listening/sound at least 4/6, and speaking task at rubric level 2 with all critical repair functions attempted.

### 13.3 Form A listening items

1. Hear `Bonsoir !` → choose the situation: evening arrival.
2. Hear `Bonne journée !` → choose: daytime leaving wish.
3. Hear spelled `M–A–Y–A` → select `Maya` from plausible options.
4. Hear one controlled `u/ou` example → choose its written form.
5. Hear one `é/è` example → choose sound group.
6. Hear `sans`, `son`, or `cinq` → choose nasal group.

Recordings must be distinct from lesson recordings while retaining reviewed speaker clarity.

### 13.4 Form A reading/context items

1. You did not understand → select `Je ne comprends pas.`
2. The speaker is too fast → select `Plus lentement, s'il vous plaît.`
3. Someone gives you the class handout → select `Merci.`
4. You need to interrupt a teacher politely → select `Excusez-moi.`

### 13.5 Form A writing items

1. Hear `I–N–È–S`; type `Inès`. Two points: ordered letters (1), accent (1).
2. English prompt “See you tomorrow!”; type `À demain !` (1.5 points: phrase 1, accent 0.5).
3. English purpose “Ask politely for repetition”; type `Pouvez-vous répéter, s'il vous plaît ?` (1.5 points: communicative phrase 1, key orthography 0.5).

### 13.6 Form A speaking/interaction scenario

**Setting:** You arrive at a French class in the evening. The teacher speaks too quickly. At the end, you leave.

Prompts:

1. “Greet your teacher.”
2. Partner audio gives a short instruction quickly. “Ask for repetition or slower speech.”
3. Partner repeats slowly and gives the learner a document. “Respond appropriately.”
4. “Leave and wish the teacher a good evening.”

Scoring dimensions, 0–2 each:

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Situation-appropriate language | Missing/inappropriate | Some correct functions | All or nearly all functions appropriate |
| Communication repair | No usable repair request | Partial/supported request | Clear repeat or slower-speech request |
| Comprehensibility | Meaning not recoverable | Meaning recoverable with effort | Meaning generally clear |
| Interaction completion | Scenario not completed | Partially completed | Completes beginning, repair, thanks, and ending |

Rubric-level mapping:

- 0–2 points: level 0.
- 3–4 points: level 1.
- 5–6 points: level 2.
- 7–8 points: level 3.

Accent similarity is not a dimension. The learner may rerecord each prompt once before final submission during the deterministic self-reviewed foundation.

### 13.7 Form B requirements

Form B keeps the same blueprint and difficulty but changes:

- Time/context combinations.
- Fictional name and spelling sequence.
- Sound example recordings.
- Courtesy/repair scenarios.
- Speaking scenario from classroom to beginner workshop/check-in desk.

Form B must not merely reorder Form A options.

### 13.8 Assessment remediation map

| Failure signal | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Day/evening or arrival/leaving confusion | L01 contextual mini-review |
| Letter/name sequence errors | L02 confused-pair and short-dictation review |
| `u/ou`, `é/è`, or nasal recognition below threshold | L03 target-specific listening set |
| Courtesy mismatch | L04 context-pair review |
| Cannot request repetition/slower speech | L05 structured repair replay and retry |
| Meaning clear but orthography weak | Short spelling/accents review; preserve communicative credit |
| Speaking task incomplete | Rehearse deterministic scenario, then attempt Form B speaking scenario |

## 14. Answer specifications

### 14.1 Shared French normalization

```yaml
unicode: NFC
trim_outer_whitespace: true
collapse_internal_whitespace: true
normalize_apostrophes: true
case_sensitive: false
terminal_punctuation: optional
diacritics:
  communication_activity: correct_with_note
  spelling_or_dictation_activity: required
```

### 14.2 Accepted-answer table

| Answer ID | Canonical | Permitted variant | Important errors |
|---|---|---|---|
| `answer.greeting.bonjour` | `Bonjour !` | terminal punctuation optional | `Bonsoir` changes time; `Au revoir` changes function |
| `answer.greeting.bonsoir` | `Bonsoir !` | terminal punctuation optional | `Bonne soirée` is normally a leaving wish |
| `answer.farewell.a-bientot` | `À bientôt !` | missing accents: correct with note outside spelling | Wrong time/function phrase |
| `answer.farewell.a-demain` | `À demain !` | missing `À` accent: correct with note outside spelling | `À bientôt` is less specific |
| `answer.courtesy.please` | `S'il vous plaît.` | curly apostrophe accepted | `S'il te plaît` is meaningful but outside target formal frame; accept with register note only when context permits informal |
| `answer.courtesy.excuse-me` | `Excusez-moi.` | terminal punctuation optional | Missing hyphen: correct with note outside spelling |
| `answer.classroom.understand` | `Je comprends.` | terminal punctuation optional | — |
| `answer.classroom.not-understand` | `Je ne comprends pas.` | terminal punctuation optional | Missing `ne` is common spoken usage but not the target beginner written form; partially correct with usage note |
| `answer.classroom.repeat` | `Pouvez-vous répéter, s'il vous plaît ?` | terminal punctuation optional; apostrophe normalized | Missing accent: correct with note outside spelling; informal `Peux-tu…` only accepted when context explicitly permits |
| `answer.classroom.slower` | `Plus lentement, s'il vous plaît.` | terminal punctuation optional | `Répétez` changes request purpose |
| `answer.classroom.what-mean` | `Qu'est-ce que ça veut dire ?` | apostrophe normalized | Word-order changes require reviewed alternative |
| `answer.classroom.how-say` | `Comment dit-on {slot} en français ?` | slot accepts displayed source word | Hyphen omission: correct with note outside spelling |

No generic fuzzy acceptance is permitted. Unrecognized but potentially correct free-text responses receive `needs_review` rather than automatic rejection in evaluated production tasks.

## 15. Review schedule initialization

After meaningful instruction, core phrases begin at Learning. The initial deterministic schedule uses:

- First short review: approximately 10 minutes or end of session.
- Delayed reviews: 1, 3, 7, 14, 30, and 60 days after successful independent recall.

Unit-specific rules:

- Greeting/farewell pairs rotate contextual prompts.
- Alphabet review targets observed confusions rather than scheduling all letters equally.
- Sound discrimination rotates recordings and example words.
- Long classroom phrases move from builder → supported recall → typed/spoken recall.
- Immediate corrected retries do not replace the next delayed review.

## 20. Editorial and cultural notes

- Teach `bonjour` as the safest daytime greeting without asserting a universal clock cutoff.
- Teach `bonsoir` as the normal evening greeting; context and local practice vary.
- Explain `salut` as informal without claiming it is rude in all other contexts.
- Present `vous` phrases as polite/formal-safe and acknowledge plural use later.
- `De rien` is a common response to thanks, not the only response.
- Do not describe one supported French variety as more correct.
- Use adult contexts and avoid childish reward language.
- Example names must remain fictional and diverse.
- No public phrase may contain a real learner's name imported from ChatGPT history.
